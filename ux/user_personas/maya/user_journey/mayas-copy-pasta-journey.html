Where to get posts, easiest first

Start with Bluesky. Mango Tree can get those posts itself, so Maya never leaves. Every other site sends her away, so Mango Tree tells her the effort up front. Effort means how much she does herself: none, low, medium, or not possible. Most sites have more than one way to get the posts. Mango Tree recommends one and keeps the others one click away.

Other ways to explore, shown in step 6

A research partner collects for her

A university or lab that already runs 4CAT collects posts for Mango Tree users. 4CAT needs a server and technical setup, which a partner would handle.

A one-click preset with a data service

A Mango Tree preset on a point-and-click service such as Apify, which has a free tier and exports a spreadsheet. Maya fills in the words, and Mango Tree does the rest.

A built-in connection to Bluesky

Mango Tree gets Bluesky posts directly, so there's no download and no drop-off. This is the cheapest place to start.
Risks
Every route needs a legal and terms-of-service review before Mango Tree recommends it. Partners add cost and a dependency. Sites change access often (CrowdTangle shut down in 2024), so this table needs an owner and a review date. Don't list a tool the team hasn't tested.

How Mango Tree spots copy-pasta

Three signals, each explained in Maya's words. One signal is worth a look. Two or three together is a strong sign. None of them proves who is behind it. Mango Tree checks all three for her; she doesn't choose.

1. Same words

"Lots of different accounts posted the same sentence, word for word."
What she sees
The sentence, and how many accounts used it.
Research
DFRLab found one slogan reply posted 14 times by different accounts in a single network.
Mango Tree does this today (exact repeats) New: also catch near-copies with a word changed

2. Same time

"They posted it within minutes of each other."
What she sees
Dots bunched together on a timeline.
Research
Schoch and colleagues (2022) count two accounts posting the same message within one minute. Together with shared reposts, this caught over 80% of a known troll campaign's accounts, with about 1% false alarms. Pacheco and colleagues used 30-minute windows. Shorter windows give fewer false matches.
New work. The times are already in her file.

3. Look-alike accounts

"The accounts look alike: made the same week, posting more than a person would, with almost no profile."
What she sees
Account details for each group.
Research
In one network, DFRLab found every account was created on one of three days. DFRLab's bot guide treats 72 posts a day as suspicious and over 144 as highly suspicious.
New work. Needs account details her file may not have.

The main picture: a timeline

Each dot is one post of the copied sentence, with one row per account. Dots stacked in a narrow band were posted at the same time. This is easier for Maya to read than a network map, which stays in an advanced view for researchers.
Illustrative example, not real data.

Advanced setting, for researchers only

Maya never sees this. Mango Tree uses Balanced for her. A researcher can change it under Advanced.
Strict
The same words, posted within 1 minute of each other. Fewest matches, strongest sign. Based on Schoch and colleagues.
Balanced (recommended)
The same or nearly the same words, posted within 30 minutes. Based on Pacheco and colleagues.
Broad
The same words at any time in the dates chosen. Most matches, weakest sign.
These are starting points to test, not settled defaults.
How a group is labeled
One signal: "Worth a look." Two signals: "Strong sign." All three: "Very strong sign." Never "bot" or "fake," because the tool can't know that.
What it can't prove
Activists, fans and campaigns also post the same words at the same time. Matching words alone are not proof of coordination (Pacheco and colleagues). Mango Tree shows the signals, and Maya decides.
